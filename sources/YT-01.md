Epistemic Honesty and the Other Minds Question in Experiential Empiricism
Brandon Sergent
Abstract
This paper clarifies a crucial ambiguity in Experiential Empiricism (EE) and Reality Repair Theory (RRT) regarding the ontological status of other minds. While EE successfully dissolves many classical problems by working directly with experiential patterns rather than assuming external realities, the question of whether other experiential patterns contain actual phenomenal consciousness remains formally unprovable. This paper argues that EE's strength lies not in proving other minds exist, but in showing that the question is operationally irrelevant. The protocol for responding to suffering reports remains identical whether other beings are genuinely conscious or merely exhibit consciousness-like behavior. This clarification maintains EE's commitment to epistemic rigor while preserving all practical and ethical implications of the framework.

The Bridge Trap: Why Mind-Merging Technology Cannot Solve the Problem of Other Minds
Brandon M. Sergent
Abstract
David Pearce proposes that reversible thalamic bridges will one day permit mind-melding between subjects and so lay the problem of other minds to rest. William Hirstein argues on independent grounds that direct access to another person's conscious states ("mindmelding") is possible and that the privacy of consciousness is a dispensable dogma. The craniopagus Hogan twins, joined by a functioning thalamic bridge, and the existing brain-to-brain interface literature are offered as early evidence. This paper argues that no bridge, at any bandwidth, can solve the problem of other minds, and that the attempt manufactures worse problems than the one it targets. The core argument is a dilemma. To the extent two bridged parties remain distinct, neither can verify that the other feels, because every signal arrives inside a single phenomenal field. To the extent they merge, the bridge has not verified an other mind but produced a third one, and a reversible bridge is by construction a mechanism for ending that third subject over its own objection. Between the horns, four further failures follow: any instrument that can confirm suffering can deny it, compulsory neural correlation replaces empathy as a moral act with empathy as an enforced state, the proposal inherits externalism's structure of making suffering secondary to measurement, and the boundary it dissolves is the very thing that makes other minds morally inviolable. Experiential Empiricism's treatment of other minds is defended as the stronger position precisely because it treats the epistemic limit as a protective boundary rather than an engineering defect.

### @Innomen

9 hours ago
 @DavidPearce1  Dave, yes, it is maddening. :) But first, to answer your question: phenomenalism traditionally reduces physical objects to actual or possible experience, but often retains some further machinery to account for the persistence and organization of those experiences. EE is more radical in its subtraction. Its sole axiom is “Experience is.” Everything else, including objects, physics, mathematics, causality, memory and other minds, must be established from experience rather than installed as an additional ontological foundation. It doesn't replace matter with another hidden substrate. It asks why we needed to posit one in the first place.

And I don't think your NMP is far away from this at all. I actually wrote a paper specifically comparing the two:

After Matter, What Is “Physical”? Non-Materialist Physicalism and Experiential Empiricism at the Final Subtraction

Abstract:

David Pearce’s non-materialist physicalism (NMP), or physicalistic idealism, is unusually close to Experiential Empiricism (EE). Both reject materialism, both take experience seriously as fundamental rather than emergent from intrinsically non-experiential matter, both preserve the mathematical and predictive apparatus of modern physics, and neither requires mathematical Platonism. The decisive difference appears only after these agreements are granted. NMP retains physicalism: reality is mind-independent, no element of reality is absent from the mathematical formalism of an adequate physics, and the intrinsic nature of the reality so described is experiential. EE stops one step earlier. Its sole axiom is “Experience is.” Physics is retained as extraordinarily successful mathematical abstraction over experiential regularities, but no further inference from predictive structure to mind-independent ontology is licensed without evidence. This paper argues that NMP therefore represents an exceptionally instructive final near-miss: it removes non-experiential matter while retaining the externalist reconstruction that generated many of the problems EE dissolves. An ontological ablation test makes the difference precise. Remove from NMP the claim that physical formalism describes a mind-independent reality while retaining every equation, observation, prediction, experiment, and phenomenal fact. If no empirical or explanatory capacity is lost, “physical” is ontologically idle. If something is lost, that loss must be identified. The comparison is especially revealing in phenomenal binding and the problem of other minds, where NMP seeks positive physical mechanisms for problems EE argues are generated by the additional ontology itself.
